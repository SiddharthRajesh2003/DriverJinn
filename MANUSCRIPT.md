## Abstract

Separating cancer driver genes from passengers is limited by cohort size in
frequency-based methods, and network-based deep learning has shown that a gene's
position in a molecular interaction network is itself predictive. Almost all such
methods, however, inherit the homophily assumption built into graph convolution —
that a gene resembles its neighbours — which is quantitatively false for drivers,
whose neighbours are themselves drivers in only 0.10 to 0.21 of cases across the
standard gene interaction networks. We present DriverJinn, which makes network
geometry explicit through discrete Ricci curvature: a per-edge scalar that separates
interactions inside redundant functional modules from the bottleneck interactions
that are the sole route between them, and the same quantity that governs
over-squashing in message passing. Curvature enters the model three ways — as fifteen
per-gene descriptors appended to the multi-omics feature vector, as a conditioning
signal inside the attention logits, and as the factorisation of a six-pathway encoder
whose learned per-gene weights report whether a prediction rests on a gene's
bottleneck or its in-module interactions. Contrastive views are generated by
randomised Schur-complement elimination, which marginalises genes out of the graph
Laplacian rather than deleting edges and so preserves effective resistance between
all retained genes; the model is trained as a ranker under a positive-unlabelled
formulation rather than as a binary classifier. Across three gene interaction
networks under 5-fold cross-validation DriverJinn attains NDCG@50 of 0.478 ± 0.062
and an 8.7-fold enrichment for COSMIC Tier 1 drivers at K = 50. Its highest-confidence
unannotated candidates converge on a single chromatin-remodelling axis spanning the
SIN3–HDAC, SWI/SNF and NuRD complexes — a computational shortlist offered for
experimental follow-up.

**Keywords:** cancer driver genes; graph neural networks; Ricci curvature;
contrastive learning; Schur complement; positive-unlabelled learning; learning to
rank.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The driver–passenger problem

A tumour genome carries thousands of somatic alterations, of which only a small
minority confer a selective growth advantage; the rest are passengers accumulated
along the way [60]. Separating the two is the central problem of cancer genomics,
and it is the step on which target selection, biomarker development and the
interpretation of an individual patient's sequencing report all depend. The
catalogues that anchor the field — the COSMIC Cancer Gene Census [28,29], the
Network of Cancer Genes [30] and the pan-cancer driver compilation of Bailey *et
al.* [31] — have grown with every release, which is itself evidence that the list
is incomplete.

The first generation of computational methods attacked the problem statistically, by
asking whether a gene is mutated more often than a background model of neutral
mutation predicts. MutSigCV [61] made this tractable by modelling the strong
covariation of background mutation rate with replication timing and expression;
OncodriveFML [62] extended the logic to functional impact and to non-coding regions;
20/20+ [63] and DriverML [64] learned the signature of driver mutation patterns from
labelled examples. These methods are well calibrated and interpretable, but they
share a structural limitation: statistical power is a function of cohort size and
mutation frequency, so genes that are drivers in a small subset of tumours, or that
act through mechanisms other than recurrent point mutation, are systematically
invisible to them. The long tail of infrequently mutated drivers is precisely the
part of the list that remains unwritten.

### 1.2 From frequency to network position

The complementary insight is that drivers are not distributed randomly through the
cell's interaction machinery. Alterations that produce the same phenotype tend to
cluster in the same pathways and complexes, so a gene's neighbourhood in a molecular
network carries information about its function that its mutation count does not;
network propagation formalises this and has become a standard amplifier of weak
genetic signal [65]. This observation motivated a line of graph-based deep learning
methods for driver prediction. EMOGI [26] integrates multi-omics features with a
graph convolutional network over a protein interaction network and provides
attribution-based interpretation of its predictions. MTGCN [27] adds link prediction
as an auxiliary task, using the network's own topology as a source of supervision to
compensate for the small number of labelled drivers. MODIG [59] builds several
gene-relation graphs and fuses them with graph attention. Across this literature the
result is consistent: representing genes relationally beats treating them
independently.

### 1.3 Three problems this literature leaves open

**Drivers are heterophilic, and graph convolution assumes they are not.** Graph
convolution and its attention variants [24] compute a node's representation by
aggregating its neighbours, an operation that is informative only when neighbours
tend to share the label — the homophily assumption. Zhang *et al.* measured this
assumption directly on the three networks used throughout this literature and found
it violated: the driver homophily ratio is 0.0951 on GGNet, 0.1853 on PathNet and
0.2085 on PPNet, all far below the 0.5 threshold at which a network is considered
homophilic, and the performance of homophily-assuming architectures degrades most on
the most heterophilic network [4]. Their solution, HGDC, is a heterophilic diffusion
convolution that constructs an auxiliary diffusion network to raise the effective
homophily ratio. That treats the symptom. The underlying fact is geometric: a driver
whose neighbours are largely non-drivers, and whose neighbours are largely unlike one
another, sits on edges whose endpoint neighbourhoods barely overlap. This is a
statement about the local curvature of the network, and it can be measured directly
rather than diffused away.

Discrete Ricci curvature does exactly this. For an edge, Ollivier–Ricci curvature
[5,6] is the normalised optimal-transport deficit between the random-walk measures at
its two endpoints [7,8]: positive when the neighbourhoods overlap so that probability
mass moves cheaply — a redundant, co-regulated module in which function is
distributed across partners — and negative when the neighbourhoods are near-disjoint
so that all flow funnels across a single edge — a bottleneck between modules.
Curvature is already established as a cancer-relevant network signal: Sandhu *et al.*
showed that Ollivier–Ricci curvature separates tumour from normal gene co-expression
networks and relates quantitatively to network robustness [1], with subsequent
applications to drug response [2] and to ovarian carcinoma prognosis [3].
Independently, and from the machine learning side, Topping *et al.* proved that
negatively curved edges are precisely the edges responsible for over-squashing, the
failure mode in which information from distant nodes is compressed past the point of
recovery [10,11]. The two results meet: curvature marks both the topological
positions where drivers are expected to sit and the positions where a conventional
GNN will fail to propagate their signal. Prior work has used curvature as a global
descriptor of a network, or as a criterion for rewiring the graph before learning
[10]. Attali *et al.* went further, constraining message passing by curvature regime
and hop depth and showing that this outperforms rewiring on node classification [12];
they close by proposing, as future work, an attention mechanism conditioned on edge
curvature rather than on node features alone. That mechanism has not been built, and
curvature has not previously been used as a per-gene predictive feature for driver
identification.

**Standard graph augmentations are biologically wrong for interaction networks.**
Contrastive learning needs two stochastic views of the input [15,16,17], and the
default graph augmentations are random edge dropping and node dropping [18]. On an
interaction network these are actively harmful: deleting a bridge edge disconnects a
functional module, so the two views disagree about pathway connectivity and the
encoder is trained toward an invariance that contradicts the biology. Zhu *et al.*
make the general form of this argument — augmentations should preserve intrinsic
structure rather than perturb uniformly [17]. The principled alternative is to
*marginalise* genes out instead of deleting edges. Kothapalli introduced randomised
Schur-complement approximation as a graph-contrastive augmentor, with theoretical
justification and an explicit connection to graph diffusion [19]; SCIS-CDG
subsequently applied this class of augmentor to cancer driver networks [20]. The
appeal is a hard guarantee: the Schur complement of a graph Laplacian is Kron
reduction [21], which is again a valid Laplacian and preserves effective resistance
[22] exactly between all retained nodes. Two such views differ in local wiring but
agree on how strongly every remaining pair of genes is connected through the network
as a whole — which is what makes them a legitimate positive pair. Notably, the
diffusion semantics this shares with HGDC's auxiliary network are the same effect
HGDC credits for its own performance gain [4], reached by a different route.

**The task is ranking under positive-unlabelled data, and it is not evaluated that
way.** A gene absent from the Cancer Gene Census may be a confirmed non-driver or
merely undiscovered, and the growth of the catalogues [30,31] shows the second case
is common. This is the positive-unlabelled setting [32,33], well recognised in
bioinformatics [34,35], yet driver methods are routinely trained as binary
classifiers and scored by AUROC. With roughly 6% positives, AUROC is dominated by the
ordering of the unlabelled majority and is known to be misleading under exactly this
imbalance [43]. It also does not match the use case. The product of such a method is
a shortlist that a laboratory will follow up experimentally, so the quantity that
matters is how many true drivers appear in the top tens of genes, with a discount for
position — which is what NDCG@K measures [42]. No published method on these networks
reports it.

### 1.4 DriverJinn

We address the three problems together. DriverJinn is a curvature-aware graph
attention network trained contrastively on Schur-complement views and optimised
directly as a ranker. Its contributions are:

1. **Curvature as an explicit, per-gene signal.** Fifteen descriptors — six
   Ollivier–Ricci statistics, six augmented Forman–Ricci statistics [13], and the
   counts of positively and negatively curved incident edges together with a
   curvature-homophily term after [12] — are appended to each gene's 58-dimensional
   multi-omics feature vector [4]. Both discretisations are retained deliberately:
   they are strongly but imperfectly correlated, and the combinatorial variant
   remains informative where optimal transport is degenerate [13].

2. **Curvature-conditioned attention.** Attention logits are a learned mixture of
   curvature-as-evidence and curvature-as-prior terms, so how much a gene attends to
   a partner depends on the topological role of the interaction connecting them and
   not only on what the partner looks like. This implements the mechanism named as
   future work in [12] and applies it to driver-gene ranking.

3. **A six-pathway encoder that is interpretable by construction.** Following the
   curvature-regime × hop-depth factorisation of [12], six independent stacks over
   {positive, negative, all} × {1-hop, 2-hop} are combined by learned per-gene
   attention. The pathway weights are directly readable: they state whether a gene's
   score derives from its bottleneck interactions or its in-module ones — the kind of
   mechanistic account this field expects of a network-based method [26,27].

4. **Structure-preserving contrastive augmentation, honestly characterised.** Views
   are drawn by randomised Schur-complement elimination following [19,20]. Because
   materialising the exact complement creates a clique per eliminated gene and is
   intractable at this scale, we use the randomised elimination of Kyng & Sachdeva
   [23]; resistance preservation therefore holds in expectation and as a spectral
   approximation with high probability, not exactly. Contrastive learning runs on the
   views and ranking on the original graph, so no prediction is ever made on a
   perturbed network and every gene remains scoreable at inference.

5. **Ranking-first training and evaluation.** The objective combines NT-Xent over the
   two views [15,16,36] with a focal-weighted BPR loss [38,39] over
   (positive, unlabelled) pairs under a hard-negative curriculum. NDCG@50 is the
   primary metric, with AUROC, AUPRC, Precision@K, Recall@K and MRR reported for
   comparability. We are explicit about one consequence of our label policy: folds
   are stratified over all genes, so the negative class pools curated non-drivers
   with unannotated genes, and the resulting AUROC/AUPRC are computed over a
   different negative population from published baselines evaluated on the annotated
   subset alone. Where like-for-like ranking comparison is required, baselines are
   rerun from their released implementations under the present protocol.

We evaluate on three networks of genuinely different construction — GGNet, a
gene–gene interaction network derived from RNA-level interactomes; PathNet, built
from KEGG and Reactome pathways; and PPNet, the STRING protein interaction network —
all taken from the HGDC release [4] so that features, labels and networks match the
published baselines. The consistency of the resulting predictions across three
networks built from different evidence types, rather than performance on any single
one, is the result we regard as most informative biologically.

The remainder of the paper describes the method (Section 2), reports
cross-validation and enrichment results together with ablations over curvature
features, attention mode and elimination strategy (Section 3), and examines the
top-ranked unannotated candidates in the light of existing cancer biology
(Section 4).
